abstract: |
  Phylogenetic diversity measures summarise how much evolutionary history a community holds and how much two communities share, but applying them to metabarcoding data requires a tree that covers the reference units samples are identified to, with branch lengths that mean the same thing throughout. For the large barcode reference libraries of animals (BOLD) and fungi (UNITE), no such tree exists, and attempts to infer one from the barcodes themselves fail at the deep end: short markers saturate, cannot be aligned across distant groups, or both. We present timegraft, a workflow that takes the topology and ages of the tree down to genus level from published syntheses (Open Tree of Life and TimeTree) and uses barcodes only within genera, where they carry reliable signal. Each genus is resolved and dated independently from its reference units, and grafted onto the dated backbone, so that the final tree is ultrametric, in millions of years, with a BIN or species hypothesis at every tip. Metabarcoding sequences are then matched to reference units or placed near their closest one, and Faith's phylogenetic diversity, mean pairwise distance, UniFrac and PhyloSor are computed per sample. Every age in the tree is traceable to its source. Applied to COI reference data for 1,232 genera of western Iberian invertebrates and fishes (25,166 BINs), timegraft built a dated tree of 24,894 tips in 36 minutes on two processor cores. TimeTree dated about a third of the backbone nodes directly; its genus crown ages were often inflated for genera that are not monophyletic, and are used only as upper bounds. Barcode distances saturate within genera, so they give a minimum crown age and not an estimate; crown ages are therefore handled as a range between that minimum and the bounds from TimeTree and the stem age, and diversity is reported at both ends of the range. Penalised likelihood dating within genera changed branch lengths little and could not estimate crown ages, and is offered as an option only. Conflicts between the two syntheses exposed errors in the Open Tree taxonomy. # Introduction

Metabarcoding has made it routine to list the taxa in an environmental sample, but lists alone ignore how those taxa are related. Two samples may share no species and yet consist of close relatives throughout, or share many species and differ deeply in the rest. Phylogenetic diversity measures address this by summarising a community on a tree: Faith's phylogenetic diversity (PD) as the total branch length connecting its members [@faith1992pd], mean pairwise distance (MPD) as the average divergence between them [@webb2002communities], and UniFrac and PhyloSor as the branch length two communities share [@lozupone2005unifrac; @lozupone2007weighted; @bryant2008phylosor]. These measures are only as meaningful as the tree's branch lengths, and they can only be computed for the taxa the tree contains.

For metabarcoding of animals and fungi, the reference units that sequences are identified to are clusters in barcode libraries: Barcode Index Numbers (BINs) in BOLD for the cytochrome *c* oxidase subunit I gene (COI) [@ratnasingham2013bin], and species hypotheses in UNITE for the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) [@koljalg2013sh; @nilsson2019unite]. There are hundreds of thousands of each, most without a place in any published phylogeny.

Building a tree for these units from the barcodes themselves is attractive, since the data are already there, but it fails in predictable ways. COI saturates: beyond a few tens of millions of years, multiple substitutions at the same sites stop pairwise distances from growing, so barcodes cannot resolve or date deep splits. ITS cannot even be aligned across distant fungal groups. Earlier work at our institute ran into exactly these limits. A divide-and-conquer approach that inferred family-level trees and a backbone of exemplars from COI [@bactria], following the logic of earlier large-tree pipelines [@antonelli2017supersmart], did not scale at the backbone, and produced backbone branch lengths that could not be reconciled with those within families. An approach that used embeddings from a DNA language model to resolve a taxonomic tree and assign its branch lengths [@evospaice; @wei2024branchlengths] showed that embedding distances tracked sequence divergence only at short distances; beyond roughly genus level, sequence distance itself had plateaued (Fig. 1).

Elsewhere, the most successful large reference trees for single-gene data have separated the two problems: the deep structure comes from a tree inferred from more informative data, and the single gene is only used to attach sequences to it. Greengenes2 placed more than 21 million 16S sequences onto a genome-based tree of bacteria and archaea [@mcdonald2024greengenes2], using a learned placement model [@jiang2023depp]. For insects, synthesis trees combine phylogenomic backbones with barcodes [@chesters2020insects; @chesters2026lepidoptera]. Placement of COI on fixed backbones is accurate for most species when the backbone is well sampled [@fernando2025placement].

timegraft applies this separation to barcode reference libraries, with two further choices. First, the backbone is not inferred at all but taken from two published syntheses: the branching order of genera from the Open Tree of Life [@hinchliff2015otol; @rees2017ott] and their divergence times from TimeTree [@kumar2022timetree5]. Second, all branch lengths are expressed in time, so that subtrees built independently fit the backbone without rescaling, and diversity is comparable across markers. Barcodes are used where they are informative: to resolve and date relationships within genera, and to place metabarcoding sequences.

Here we describe the method, its implementation as a Snakemake workflow [@molder2021snakemake], and a first application to COI reference data for the genera recorded in western Iberia, and we discuss what the sources of ages contribute and where they fail.

# Methods

## Overview

timegraft builds a dated tree in four stages (Fig. 2): (i) one representative sequence per reference unit; (ii) a dated backbone with one tip per genus; (iii) one dated subtree per genus; and (iv) grafting of the subtrees onto the backbone. Metabarcoding sequences are then matched or placed, and diversity computed. Steps that depend on the marker (filtering, distances, crown ages, dating and placement settings) are configurable plug-ins; COI and ITS settings are provided. The workflow is implemented in Python with DendroPy [@sukumaran2010dendropy] for tree handling and Snakemake for orchestration.

## Reference units

Records are read from a BOLD data package or a table of record identifier, unit, genus and sequence. COI records are kept if they meet a minimum length, contain few ambiguous bases, and have a reading frame without stop codons under the appropriate mitochondrial genetic code. Because a taxonomically broad data set spans several codes, the code is chosen per record: the most specific of the record's genus, family, order, class or phylum that occurs in a mapping derived from the NCBI Taxonomy decides it, with a default for records that match nothing. For each unit, the representative is the medoid record by *k*-mer distance [@ondov2016mash], which requires no alignment. A unit is assigned to the genus that at least 80% of its identified records agree on; units without a genus, or with conflicting identifications, are set aside for placement.

Because within-genus distances bound the crown age of a genus (below), units that do not belong to their genus are screened out before genus subtrees are built. All representatives are searched against each other with VSEARCH [@rognes2016vsearch] (identity at least 80%, query coverage at least 70%). A unit is set aside if it is at least two percentage points more similar to a unit of another genus than to any unit of its own genus, or if no unit of its own genus reaches 80% identity while other units of the genus do. Genera with a single unit are not screened, and a genus never loses all its units. Set-aside units are placed like units without a genus (see Placement), or, if nothing reaches the placement threshold, on the stem of their own genus halfway between its crown and stem ages, so that they remain in the tree and in diversity without determining the crown age.

## Dated backbone

Genera are matched by name to the Open Tree Taxonomy within a context taxon (for example Metazoa) to avoid homonyms across kingdoms. The topology is induced from the labelled synthetic tree: a genus that is a node in the synthesis becomes a tip there; otherwise it is attached at the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of its species in the synthesis, or failing that at its nearest enclosing taxon that is.

Each internal node is dated by asking TimeTree for the age of the MRCA of a few genera from each side of it, keyed by NCBI taxon identifiers. Because TimeTree knows many species but not every genus, each genus is represented together with some of its species; other genera and species are tried when TimeTree knows none of the first picks on one side, and as a last resort the enclosing taxa of a side (from family up to phylum) are used, but only taxa that contain no genus from the other side, so that the MRCA is still the same node. An age is accepted only if TimeTree found taxa on at least two sides of the node.

When a node is not younger than its nearest dated ancestor, equal ages are read as TimeTree not resolving the node, a direct age takes precedence over one obtained through enclosing taxa, and otherwise the ancestor's age is kept. Remaining undated nodes are placed between their nearest dated ancestor and oldest dated descendant by their expected height under a pure-birth model, (*H<sub>n</sub>* − 1)/λ for a clade of *n* lineages with *H<sub>n</sub>* the *n*-th harmonic number, which amounts to fitting λ to each pair of dated nodes. Here *n* is the number of genera in the corresponding clade of the synthetic tree, including genera absent from the data, so that the age of a clade does not depend on which other genera are sampled. Spacing them evenly, as in BLADJ [@webb2008phylocom], is an option. Each genus tip then has a stem age *S*.

## Genus subtrees

For each genus with at least two units, the units and ten outgroup units, taken in turn from the genera of the three nearest sister clades in the backbone (longest sequences first), are aligned with MAFFT [@katoh2013mafft], and pairwise distances computed under the Kimura two-parameter model [@kimura1980k2p] (for ITS, *k*-mer distances without alignment). A BioNJ tree [@gascuel1997bionj] is rooted on the edge that leaves the fewest units on the wrong side of the root (minimum violation), and the outgroup is then removed. Units of the genus that fall among the outgroup stay in the tree as its earliest branches and are recorded, as some of them will be misidentified. Ties between edges are resolved by minimal ancestor deviation (MAD) on the genus tree [@tria2017mad], which also roots genera without an outgroup.

The crown age *C* of the genus is a point age from a user-supplied table of literature values where there is one. Otherwise it is handled as a range. The upper bound is the smallest of 0.9 *S*, the TimeTree age of the MRCA of up to twelve of the genus's species, and a maximum from the table, which replaces the TimeTree bound for that genus. The TimeTree age is an upper bound only: for a monophyletic genus it approximates the crown age, but for a genus that is not monophyletic it is the age of a much deeper node, so it can lower an age but should never raise one. The lower bound is the age from the barcodes, the mean root-to-tip distance of the rooted genus tree divided by a substitution rate (by default 0.0115 substitutions per site per million years per lineage, the commonly used 2.3% pairwise divergence per million years for arthropod mitochondrial DNA [@brower1994], which lies within the range of COI rates calibrated on splits of a few million years [@knowlton1998rates; @loezaquintana2019clock]), raised by a minimum from the table where given. Because distances saturate, also within genera (see Results), an age from such a rate is a minimum. The rate can be set per taxon, and is resolved for each genus from the most specific of its genus, family, order, class or phylum for which one is given.

*C* is then either the lower bound (the default) or the midpoint of the range on a logarithmic scale, the geometric mean of the two bounds, which is the choice with the smallest worst-case relative error when only the bounds are known. Both bounds are recorded per genus. Unlike a rule that accepts or rejects the TimeTree age at a threshold, this keeps *C* a continuous function of the barcode distances. For markers without a usable rate there is no lower bound, and *C* is a stated fraction of *S* within the upper bound.

The subtree is made ultrametric with its root at *C* by mean path length [@britton2002mpl] or, optionally, by least-squares dating with LSD2 [@to2016lsd]; both assume a clock within the genus. Penalised likelihood [@sanderson2002pl], which lets the rate vary over the tree, is available through `chronos` [@paradis2013chronos] in ape [@paradis2019ape] but is not the default, for reasons given in the Results. treePL [@smith2012treepl] was not considered further because it is not available from the package channels the workflow relies on and genus trees are small.

## Evaluation of crown age estimation

Five questions were examined on the genus trees of the first application, rooted but not dated and built before screening. (i) Can penalised likelihood estimate a crown age that is only known to lie in a range? For genera with a TimeTree crown age and at least four units, the crown was bounded between half and twice that age and `chronos` was run from three random starting points under correlated, relaxed and strict-clock models. (ii) With the crown age fixed, how much do node ages from penalised likelihood differ from those of mean path length? This was measured as the ratio of total branch length, the phylogenetic diversity of the genus, on 200 randomly chosen genera of 4 to 60 units, with models compared by the information criterion ΦIC [@paradis2013chronos]. (iii) Can a substitution rate be calibrated on the data? For every genus with a TimeTree crown age younger than *S*, the implied rate is its mean root-to-tip distance divided by that age; rates per class were taken as medians, recomputed without genera more than three times above or below the median until stable, and examined against crown age and against published rates. (iv) Which age between the barcode minimum and 0.9 *S* agrees best with TimeTree crown ages: the minimum, a fraction of *S*, the median crown age of a pure-birth clade with stem age *S* and as many tips as the genus has units (birth rate at its maximum-likelihood value), the midpoint, or a regression on distance, *S* and number of units? Fitted quantities were fitted leaving each genus out. (v) How much does the phylogenetic diversity of a sample depend on crown ages? Faith's PD was computed for random samples of units on trees with every genus crown at its lower bound, at the midpoint and at its upper bound; these trees follow from the dated tree by rescaling each genus subtree, since mean path length places nodes at fixed fractions of the crown age, with placed units on a genus stem keeping their relative position between crown and stem. Sample PD was computed on the screened tree.

## Grafting

Each genus tip of the backbone is replaced by a stem of length *S* − *C* followed by its dated subtree, which keeps the tree ultrametric. Genera with a single unit keep their tip, relabelled with the unit. Genera without units are pruned. There is no global tree search, and genera are processed independently and in parallel.

## Placement and diversity

Units without a genus and metabarcoding amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) are compared with the reference records by global alignment with VSEARCH [@rognes2016vsearch], which accommodates fragments such as the Leray region of COI [@leray2013primers]. An ASV with at least 97% identity to a unit is counted as that unit; equally good matches to several units attach at their MRCA. Below that, down to 80% identity, the sequence is attached on the path from its nearest unit to the root, at the age implied by its divergence and the substitution rate (COI) or halfway along the nearest unit's terminal branch (ITS). Faith's PD, MPD (presence and abundance-weighted), unweighted and weighted UniFrac, and PhyloSor are computed from a single edge-by-sample summary of the tree. PD and MPD are also reported with every genus crown at its lower and at its upper bound, so that the part of a value that rests on crown ages is visible.

## Validation of the informative window

To measure how deep a marker carries signal, pairs of units are sampled per taxonomic stratum (same genus, same family, same order, deeper), and each barcode distance is compared with the patristic distance in a reference tree inferred from independent data, by rank correlation per stratum and by the reference distance at which the binned barcode distance reaches 90% of its maximum.

## First application

We applied timegraft to COI reference data for the genera recorded in western Iberia. From the BOLD public data package of March 2026 we selected records of Annelida, Mollusca, Arthropoda, Echinodermata, Cnidaria, Bryozoa, Porifera and Chordata from Portugal and Spain, excluding insects, arachnids, springtails, millipedes, centipedes and tetrapods, to obtain a list of genera, and then all COI-5P records of those genera worldwide, at most ten per BIN. The backbone was built from Open Tree of Life synthesis 16.1 and OTT 3.7.3 with Metazoa as context. Crown ages were set at the lower bound, with the default rate for all genera, and literature crown ages were supplied for three genera: a point age for *Gammarus* [@hou2011gammarus] and *Niphargus* [@mcinerney2014niphargus], and a fossil minimum for *Daphnia* [@kotov2011daphnia]. The run used two processor cores.

# Results

The data comprised 102,794 records of 25,166 BINs in 1,232 genera; the largest genera were the groundwater amphipod *Niphargus* (1,035 BINs) and *Gammarus* (907). The full workflow completed in 36 minutes. The final tree had 24,909 tips, was ultrametric with its root at 750 million years, and comprised 1,036 genus subtrees and 147 single-unit genera. Of 215 units without a usable genus, 210 were placed next to their nearest unit.

Most units fitted their genus: for 91% of the units of genera with at least two units, the nearest unit in the data was a congener. Screening set aside 1,294 units in 508 genera, 850 because they were closer to another genus and 444 because they were isolated within their own; 60 units were kept because their whole genus would otherwise have been removed. Of the units closer to another genus, 429 shared a family with it, 276 an order and 126 a class, and 19 shared no class; 12 units were at least 95% identical to a unit of another order or class, such as a *Balanus* BIN at 95% to the octocoral *Leptogorgia*. Single-record BINs made up 57% of the set-aside units against 35% of all units. Of the set-aside units, 819 were placed next to their nearest unit, 474 on the stem of their own genus and one could not be placed. Screening made rooting more stable (below), and in the 488 genera that lost units it lowered the mean root-to-tip distance by a median of 7%, by more than a quarter in 96 genera, and the median crown age from 8.9 to 8.2 million years. It did not change the relation between distance and crown age (below): against TimeTree crown ages, the lower bound was at a median of 0.32 times the TimeTree age before and after screening, and the midpoint within a factor of two for 75% and 73% of genera. Sample PD was unchanged for samples with one unit per genus and 8% higher with five units per genus, entirely through the placement of set-aside units on genus stems.

Of the 1,218 genera that kept units after filtering, 996 were nodes in the synthetic tree, 149 were attached at the MRCA of their species and 38 at an enclosing taxon; 35 could not be matched unambiguously in the taxonomy. TimeTree dated 261 of 779 backbone nodes directly and 53 through enclosing taxa (Table 1). A further 57 nodes received the same age as their dated ancestor, meaning TimeTree does not resolve them, and 76 TimeTree ages conflicted with an ancestor's age and were dropped; 332 nodes were interpolated. Hiding TimeTree ages in random halves and interpolating them again, pure birth on clade sizes gave ages a median factor 1.30 from TimeTree's with a median bias of 1.03, against 1.26 and 1.00 for even spacing; counting only the genera in the data made two-genus nodes 0.72 of their TimeTree age, and counting species 1.39. When a random half of the genera was dropped, 90% of interpolated node ages moved by less than a factor 1.20 with pure birth on clade sizes and 1.59 with even spacing. Relative to even spacing, pure birth made the backbone 6% longer in total.

Before screening, of the 1,046 genera with a subtree, 305 had a TimeTree crown age, of which 43 were not younger than the stem age. Many were far older than any barcode-based estimate, whether above the stem age (*Stenasellus*, 289 million years) or below it (*Haliclona*, 444), as expected for genera that are not monophyletic.

Penalised likelihood could not estimate crown ages from a range. Over 105 genera, the crown ages from three starting points differed by a median factor of 1.19 under the correlated model (1.14 relaxed, 1.19 strict clock) at practically the same penalised likelihood: with all tips at the present and only bounds on the root, rate and time are not separable, and the optimiser stays near where it started. With the crown age fixed, the correlated model gave a within-genus phylogenetic diversity at a median of 1.005 times that of mean path length, within 10% in 185 of 200 genera; the relaxed model overfitted (median 0.74), and ΦIC preferred the strict clock in all 200 genera. We therefore kept mean path length as the default and fix the crown age beforehand.

A substitution rate could not be calibrated on these data. Over the 262 genera with a TimeTree crown age below the stem age, the mean root-to-tip distance grew far less than in proportion to crown age: its median was 0.040 for the 25 genera with a crown age below 5 million years and 0.128 for the 18 above 80 million years, so that the implied rate fell from 0.016 to 0.001 substitutions per site per million years per lineage. The largest distance within a genus had a median of 0.25 and was, in half of the genera, as large as the distance to the outgroup units. The rates of the youngest genera are in the range of COI rates calibrated on splits of about three million years [@knowlton1998rates; @loezaquintana2019clock], and the decline is that expected from saturation and from the time dependency of rate estimates [@ho2005timedependency], compounded by error in the TimeTree ages. Medians per class (0.0043 for 117 teleost genera, 0.0041 for 33 gastropod, 0.0029 for 32 malacostracan and 0.0014 for 12 elasmobranch genera, the last in line with the slow mitochondrial evolution of sharks [@martin1992sharks]) were therefore two to seven times below published rates. A tree built with them had a median crown age of 24.8 million years and 129 genera at 0.9 *S*, as often where *S* came from TimeTree as where it was interpolated, against a median of 9.4 million years and 5 such genera with the default rate; we did not adopt them. Conversely, the default rate gave 12 to 13 million years for *Gammarus*, *Niphargus* and *Daphnia*, whose published crown ages are 61, 87 and at least 145 million years [@hou2011gammarus; @mcinerney2014niphargus; @kotov2011daphnia]. An age from the barcodes at a rate from young splits is thus a lower bound.

Between that lower bound and 0.9 *S*, the midpoint agreed best with TimeTree crown ages: it was at a median of 0.90 times the TimeTree age and within a factor of two for 75% of the 262 genera (0.96 and 76% for the 168 genera that were clades in the barcode tree), against 0.32 and 26% for the lower bound, 1.39 and 69% for half the stem age, and 2.05 and 49% for the birth model. A regression fitted to the data did no better (0.88 and 74%), and its coefficients, 0.47 for log distance and 0.60 for log stem age, were close to the equal weights of a geometric mean.

Crown ages mattered less for samples than for the tree as a whole, because a unit that is the only one of its genus in a sample is connected by a path of length *S* whatever the crown age. In random samples of 100 genera, Faith's PD was the same on all three trees with one unit per genus; with two and five units per genus it was 4% and 12% higher at the midpoint than at the lower bound, with rank correlations between samples of 1.00 and 0.98, and 21% and 58% higher at the upper bound (rank correlations 0.94 and 0.80).

In the final tree, the crown age was the lower bound for 1,010 genera (median 8.3 million years), was lowered to the TimeTree age for 21 and to 0.9 *S* for 2, and came from the literature table for three; the literature ages of *Gammarus* and *Niphargus* exceeded the stem ages in the backbone (53 and 23 million years) and were cut to 0.9 *S*. The upper bound was set by the stem age for 780 genera, by TimeTree for 254 and by the table for 2, and was a median of 7.3 times the lower bound. The median crown age was 8.2 million years and the total branch length of the tree 349,277 million years; with every crown at the midpoint these would be 20.6 and 554,781, and at the upper bound 58.5 and 1,334,343.

All 1,036 genus subtrees were rooted on their outgroup: 670 with the genus as a clade, and 366 with units on the wrong side of the root (median 2, at most 8). In 159 genera, 276 units of the genus fell among the outgroup, against 579 units in 351 genera before screening. Requiring the genus to be a clade with respect to two units of its sister genus, as in an earlier version, failed for 194 genera (19%); this was explained by the genus and its sister genus overlapping in barcode distance, not by saturation or errors in the backbone, and in a benchmark on simulated genera with known roots the tolerant rule was at least as accurate. Crown ages under the two rooting rules agreed (median ratio 1.00, compared under the earlier crown age rule), but 59 genera moved by more than 25%. Over the plausible roots of a genus, the crown age varied by more than a quarter in 50 genera, against 160 before screening.

Conflicts between the two syntheses pointed to errors in either. The largest involved flatfishes, crabs and freshwater snails; one was an outright taxonomic error, in which the gastropod genus *Onchidella* was placed among the sea urchins because two homonymous records had been merged in the taxonomy.

# Discussion

[TODO: develop. Points to make:]

- **Separating deep and shallow structure works at scale.** Building a tree of 25,000 BINs takes little more than half an hour without any global tree search, and the result is in a unit that is meaningful across markers.
- **The ages are the weak point, not the topology within genera.** TimeTree's coverage of marine invertebrates is patchy, interpolation is crude, and crown ages from TimeTree can only serve as upper bounds because non-monophyletic genera inflate them.
- **Barcodes bound crown ages; they do not estimate them.** Distances within genera are close to saturation in most genera, so a rate from young splits gives a minimum, and a rate fitted to older crown ages is not a rate. Marine invertebrates have few fossils, so this cannot be calibrated away. Treating the crown age as a range, with a stated convention for the age within it and diversity reported at both ends, needs no calibration points and carries over to markers such as ITS, where distances are alignment-free and saturate sooner; there the range has no lower bound and the estimate is a fraction of the stem age, which predicted TimeTree crown ages nearly as well as the barcodes did.
- **Which age within the range.** The midpoint agreed better with TimeTree than the lower bound, but TimeTree crown ages are an imperfect reference, and for old, species-rich genera with published ages even the midpoint is too young. [TODO: decide the default; the results here use the lower bound.] For samples with few units per genus the choice hardly affects diversity; for samples rich in congeners it does, and the range should be reported.
- **Why not penalised likelihood.** Rate smoothing is the standard answer to rate variation, and a reader may expect it here. Within a genus it has no calibration to work with other than the crown itself, cannot therefore estimate that crown age, and with the crown fixed reproduces mean path length to within a few per cent while its information criterion prefers the clock. Applied to the whole tree it is not defined, since the backbone has ages but no substitution branch lengths.
- **Syntheses check each other.** Disagreements between Open Tree topology and TimeTree ages flagged real errors, which suggests a use for timegraft beyond diversity: auditing both resources.
- **Provenance matters.** Because every age is recorded with its source, users can see which parts of a diversity estimate rest on direct calibrations and which on interpolation or an assumed rate.
- **Identification errors are a local problem.** Screening found few gross contaminants, and the units it set aside were mostly closer to another genus of the same family, which points to genera that are not natural groups as much as to misidentification; screening cannot tell the two apart. Removing these units steadied rooting and lowered inflated crown ages in about a hundred genera, but left the saturation of distances within genera, and with it the gap between barcode and TimeTree ages, as it was.
- **Limitations.** Genera that are not monophyletic violate the assumption of one subtree per genus, and a genus often overlaps its relatives in barcode distance, so its root is uncertain where many units fall on the wrong side; distance-based placement is less accurate than likelihood-based placement [@fernando2025placement; @matsen2010pplacer; @barbera2019epang; @balaban2020apples]; BINs and UNITE species hypotheses are delimited differently, so tips are not equivalent across markers.
- **Extension to ITS** requires extraction of ITS1 and ITS2 [@bengtsson2013itsx], alignment-free distances within genera, and crown ages without a reliable substitution rate; existing placement tools for fungi rely on alignable loci [@carbone2017tbas].
